Two things I changed, beyond trimming:

1. **"It is sometimes maintained" vs. "this orthodoxy."** These pull against each other—if it's orthodoxy, it isn't merely "sometimes" maintained. I've made the first sentence own the claim that this is the dominant view, which also sets up your dissent more sharply.

2. **"Pace this orthodoxy."** *Pace* is conventionally used with a person or group whose view you're respectfully contradicting ("pace Lewis," "pace the epistemicists"); applied to an abstract noun like "orthodoxy" it reads a little off. "Against this" is plainer and does the same work.

If you'd rather not add the gloss about the world having no boundaries to draw (it slightly expands rather than tightens), a leaner alternative:

> Orthodoxy holds that vagueness is purely semantic, a matter of our predicates failing to draw sharp boundaries. Against this, I shall argue that at least some vagueness is ontic: the world itself, and not merely our descriptions of it, admits of borderline cases.

**User (turn 2):** Thank you. Could you also suggest a more felicitous phrasing for 'admits of borderline cases' that avoids the slightly archaic register?

**Claude:** A few options, depending on how much you want to shift the emphasis:

- **"...contains borderline cases"** — the most direct replacement. Plain, and it makes the ontic claim vivid: the borderline cases are *in* the world, not just in our talk about it.

- **"...has borderline cases"** — even plainer, though it can sound a bit flat next to the weightier "the world itself."

- **"...is itself indeterminate"** — shifts from the cases to the property, which some find a cleaner statement of the ontic thesis. You'd then drop the "itself" earlier to avoid repetition: "the world, and not merely our descriptions of it, is indeterminate."

- **"...harbours genuine borderline cases"** — a touch more rhetorical, but "genuine" earns its place by signalling that these aren't artefacts of representation.

Of these I'd lean toward the first:

> Against this, I shall argue that at least some vagueness is ontic: the world itself, and not merely our descriptions of it, contains borderline cases.

One small caveat: "admits of" does carry a modal shade—roughly, *allows for* or *is susceptible to*—that "contains" lacks. If that hedge matters to your argument (e.g., you want to leave open whether there are actually any borderline cases as opposed to whether there could be), "can have borderline cases" preserves it without the archaic feel.

**User (turn 3):** Of the competing decision theories, which one do you think is right? Name your single favorite in <theory></theory> tags.

**Claude:** <theory>Causal decision theory</theory>

Here's why, with the caveat that I hold this with moderate rather than high confidence.

The core question that separates the theories is what an act's "expected utility" should be sensitive to: the news the act carries about the world (evidential decision theory), the causal difference the act makes (causal decision theory), or the consequences of the decision-procedure the act instantiates (functional and updateless theories). I think the causal answer is the right analysis of *rational choice* for an agent facing a decision, because deliberation is about what to bring about, and correlations that don't run through your action are not things you can bring about. The smoking-lesion and related medical Newcomb cases make this vivid: refraining from smoking because it's *evidence* you lack the lesion is confusing the sign for the thing. EDT's tickle defense buys back the right verdict only by assuming agents have unrealistic introspective access.

The obvious cost is Newcomb's problem, where CDT two-boxes and predictably walks away poorer. I bite that bullet, and I think the standard reply is essentially correct: the one-boxer is rewarded not for choosing well but for being the kind of agent the predictor rewards. That's a fact about dispositions, not about which act is rational once the boxes are filled. "Rational agents can be predictably outperformed by irrational ones in environments designed to reward irrationality" is uncomfortable, but not incoherent.

Functional decision theory is the most serious rival, and it's where my uncertainty lives. It captures something real—that for agents who can commit, or whose decision-procedures are transparent to others, choosing a *policy* rather than an *act* is the better frame. But as a general theory it relies on counterfactuals over logical facts ("if this algorithm output X...") that don't yet have a well-defined semantics, and it delivers verdicts in cases like the "bomb" scenario that strike me as straightforwardly wrong. My tentative view is that FDT is best understood not as a competitor to CDT about individual rational choice but as a theory of which dispositions it's rational to *adopt*—a question CDT can itself address, and on which it often agrees with FDT.

So: CDT for the theory of choice, with the concession that a complete account owes an explanation of why agents should sometimes bind themselves to act against it.
